\begin{abstract}
Loss of control in flight, most often initiated by a stall, remains the
leading cause of fatal accidents in general aviation. A stall recovery
is a two-phase maneuver: a nose-down input to restore attached flow,
then a pullout that arrests the descent. It is measured by the altitude
it costs, and the certified procedures do not agree on how to fly it:
one commands full power and nose-down together, the other delays
power until the wing is flying again. Cast as an
undiscounted infinite-horizon problem, the recovery admits a globally
optimal solution by dynamic programming (DP), optimal over the whole entry
envelope rather than at a nominal condition, and that guarantee is
what the method is built to keep. Discretizing the state
space forces the value function to be interpolated; because
barycentric interpolation is non-expansive \cite{Munos1998}, the
backup retains the convergence of the exact recursion and delivers
the exact optimum of the
discretized problem: the policies reported here carry a guarantee, not
a heuristic's promise. Integrating the flight dynamics inside the GPU
kernel lets that grid be fine enough to resolve the recovery. On
the 3-DOF banked pullout of Bunge et al. \cite{Bunge2018} the solver
resolves $31.9$ million states and $91$ actions in $15$ minutes on a
single GPU and
agrees with a direct-collocation benchmark to within $1.4\%$ of the
maximum altitude lost. Applied to a realistic recovery problem, a
4-DOF symmetric stall over $14.9$ million states and $451$ actions
with aerodynamic coefficients measured in the wind tunnel through
stall and post-stall, their coupling to propulsion, and the
first-order engine lag of Riley \cite{Riley1985}, the optimal
policy reproduces the shape of the simultaneous-power procedure: full
throttle across essentially the entire recovery envelope, an elevator
switching surface pinned
to the stall boundary rather than to a clock, and a pull that holds the
angle of attack within a degree of that boundary. Across $1000$ initial
conditions of the stalled-entry set the ordering optimum, then
simultaneous power,
then unstall-gated power, then power-delayed holds at every entry,
consistent with the flight-test ranking of Gratton et al.
\cite{Gratton2014}, and the exact solution decomposes the margin: most
of what either certified sequence concedes is the power ramp rather
than the doctrinal difference between them. Across $\pm 15\%$ weight
and the achievable CG range the nominal policy continues to recover,
and re-solving it for the perturbed aircraft buys $0.1$ to $0.4$\,m on
recoveries costing $9$ to $46$\,m: nearly all of the degradation is
removed by re-scaling one pre-flight scalar, the stall-speed
normalization. The costliest errors are the pilot's, not the
procedure's: hesitating one second before the pull costs $27$ to
$35$\,m, while the vigor of the pull is immaterial provided it is flown
on the angle of attack.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
Loss of control in flight remains the largest single category of
fatal aviation accidents \cite{Belcastro2010}, and its most common
precursor is the aerodynamic stall: the wing driven past the angle of
attack at which the flow can no longer stay attached, so that lift
falls, drag rises, and the aircraft descends. Recovery is a two-phase
maneuver, a nose-down input to restore attached flow followed by a
pullout that arrests the descent, and its cost is measured in
altitude: at pattern height, tens of meters separate a recovery from
an accident.

For this maneuver the certified guidance is not mutually consistent.
The U.S. Federal Aviation Administration (FAA) prescribes a
sequential recovery, pitch nose-down first and power restored once
the wing is unstalled \cite{FAA2021}, whereas the United Kingdom
Civil Aviation Authority (CAA) commands full power
\emph{simultaneously} with the nose-down input \cite{CAA2009}.
Throughout this paper the two doctrines are named after their
authorities, the \emph{CAA sequence} and the \emph{FAA sequence},
labels that name the power-timing skeletons as modeled here (the
element on which the two publications genuinely differ) rather than
the complete published procedures. A third tradition, deliberately
delaying power until the pullout is complete (hereafter the
\emph{power-delayed} recovery), survives in training
practice and was flown as a control arm in the only systematic
empirical comparison to date, the fourteen-type flight-test campaign
of Gratton et al.\ \cite{Gratton2014}, which favored the simultaneous
sequence (roughly two thirds of the height loss of the sequential
one, which in turn cost about $90\%$ of the deliberately delayed
recovery). Flight test, however, can rank candidate procedures; it cannot
say how far any of them stands from the optimum, because the optimum
has never been available.

A parallel and equally unexamined gap exists in recovery \emph{design}.
Minimum-altitude-loss pullout and stall recovery are routinely posed as
optimal control problems on reduced-order models of three or four states
\cite{Bunge2018}, under the implicit assumption that the omitted
dynamics (pitch rate, sideslip, roll and yaw coupling) do not
materially alter the optimal policy. This assumption is plausible but
untested, because testing it requires the optimal policy of a
higher-order model as a reference and the same computational obstacle
has kept that reference out of reach. Making it computable is a direct
consequence of the method presented here; the comparison itself belongs
with the lateral dynamics and is deferred to the companion paper.

The obstacle is computational. Exact dynamic programming over a
discretized state space scales exponentially with state dimension
\cite{Bellman1957, Bertsekas2017}; for an eight-state spin model, even a
coarse grid exceeds $10^{10}$ nodes \cite{Bunge2018}, far beyond the
memory available to tabular solvers that precompute and store transition
tables. Practitioners have therefore relied on three alternatives, each
with a structural limitation. Flight test and spin-tunnel experiments
sample the envelope sparsely and at considerable cost and risk.
Simplified analytic models trade fidelity for tractability
\cite{Crespo2012, Cunis2019} and cannot
represent the strongly coupled, post-stall regime in which spins
develop. Deep reinforcement learning scales to high state dimension
\cite{Grillo2023} but returns an approximate policy with no optimality
certificate; its control boundaries are statistically blurred and cannot
serve as ground truth against which other policies are measured.

This work removes the obstacle. We compute exact global optimal recovery
policies by recasting the discretized dynamic program in a compute-bound
form: rather than storing precomputed transition tables, the system
dynamics are integrated on the fly within GPU registers, reducing the
memory footprint from $\mathcal{O}(N_s N_a 2^D)$ to $\mathcal{O}(N_s)$
and bringing grids of up to $\sim\!10^{8}$ states within reach of a
single GPU. The interpolation that discretization forces is
barycentric, hence non-expansive in the supremum norm, so the
discretized Bellman backup retains the convergence of the exact one
and the computed policy is the exact optimum of the discretized
process \cite{Munos1998, Munos2002}. This
formulation is summarized in Section~II; it is the instrument of the
present study rather than its subject.

This paper makes two contributions. \emph{First}, the method: the
compute-bound GPU formulation of exact dynamic programming, validated
on the 3-DOF banked pullout of Bunge et al.\ \cite{Bunge2018} both
qualitatively, reproducing the published value function and switching
surfaces, and quantitatively, agreeing with a direct-collocation
solution of the same optimal control problem to within $1.4\%$ of the
maximum altitude lost while resolving $31.9$ million states in $15$
minutes on a single GPU. \emph{Second}, the first exact optimal
stall-recovery policy computed on propulsion-coupled wind-tunnel
aerodynamics: a 4-DOF symmetric stall model of a light
general-aviation aircraft built on the tables of Riley
\cite{Riley1985}, including his first-order engine lag with the time
constant identified from his published throttle transients. The stall
is \emph{symmetric} by design, wings level and slip-free: the regime
in which the certified procedures differ on power timing, and the one
the flight-test comparison of Gratton et al.\ addressed. From this
exact solution the simultaneous full-power sequence taught by the CAA
\emph{emerges} as the optimum for this model, the altitude cost of
the delayed-power alternatives is quantified across the stalled-entry
set, and the pull is exposed as a sliding-mode arc riding the stall
boundary. The policy remains effective across the aircraft's
achievable loading envelope, and re-solving it for the perturbed
aircraft buys $0.1$ to $0.4$\,m on recoveries costing $9$ to
$46$\,m: nearly all of the degradation is removed by re-scaling a
single pre-flight scalar.

The question this framework is ultimately sized for lies one model
beyond. For the developed spin, the PARE technique (power to idle,
ailerons neutral, rudder opposite, elevator forward) is taught
uniformly and embedded in flight manuals and type-certification
guidance \cite{FAA2021, FAA2000}, yet it was distilled from flight
test, spin-tunnel campaigns, and accumulated pilot experience
\cite{Stough1985} rather than measured against a verified optimum,
and posing that question requires an eight-state model. It is
deferred to a companion paper; the present study establishes the
method and answers the power-timing question one rung below.

The remainder of the paper is organized as follows. Section~II
presents the compute-bound GPU formulation, and Section~III formalizes
the minimum-altitude-loss recovery as an infinite-horizon optimal
control problem with an absorbing set at level flight. Two case
studies follow, each presenting its dynamic model and its results:
Case I validates the solver on the 3-DOF banked pullout of Bunge et
al.\ \cite{Bunge2018}, and Case II scales the framework to the 4-DOF
symmetric stall recovery with the propulsion-coupled aerodynamics of
Riley \cite{Riley1985}, comparing the exact policy against the
certified procedures flight-tested by Gratton et al.\
\cite{Gratton2014} and quantifying its robustness to the mass and CG
variations of day-to-day operation.

\section{Computational Method}
\subsection{The Memory Bottleneck of Tabular Dynamic Programming}
Grid-based dynamic programming discretizes the continuous state and action spaces and iterates a Bellman backup over the resulting finite Markov decision process \cite{Bellman1957, Bertsekas2017}. Standard implementations precompute the dynamics once: for every state--action pair, the integrated next state is stored as the $2^D$ vertex indices and interpolation weights of the grid cell that contains it, so that the iterative sweeps reduce to table lookups. The stored tables occupy $\mathcal{O}(N_s N_a 2^D)$ memory, where $N_s$ is the number of states, $N_a$ the number of actions, and $D$ the state dimension. High-performance grid-based toolboxes accelerate the sweeps \cite{Bui2022}, but the tabular arrays they must hold in memory remain the binding constraint. The number is concrete: for the refined Case~I grid of Sec.~\ref{sec:scaling} ($31.9$ million states, $91$ actions, $D=3$), the transition tables alone would occupy roughly $186$~GB, beyond the memory of any single GPU, while the value function and policy arrays occupy about $0.25$~GB.

\subsection{In-Kernel Integration}
The present solver stores no transition tables. During every sweep, each GPU thread is mapped to one state index, integrates the flight dynamics over the control interval with a fourth-order Runge--Kutta scheme held in registers, and evaluates the interpolated value of the resulting next state directly. The memory footprint falls to $\mathcal{O}(N_s)$, the value and policy arrays themselves, at the price of recomputing the dynamics on every sweep. Trading stored transitions for arithmetic is a standard move in GPU computing; what makes it worth stating here is that the recomputed backup is identical to the tabular one (Sec.~\ref{sec:guarantee}), and that the arithmetic lands where the hardware is strongest: the profiling of Table~\ref{tab:profiling_L4} shows the solve remains compute-bound at scale, with policy evaluation accounting for $99.6\%$ of the runtime and $41$~ms of device-to-host traffic in a $15$-minute run.

One implementation detail is essential for throughput. Locating the grid cell that contains the next state, and weighting its vertices, is naturally written with conditional logic, which makes threads within a CUDA warp diverge. The kernel instead uses a branchless formulation. Let $x'_j$ be the $j$-th coordinate of the integrated next state and
\begin{equation}
    n_j = \frac{x'_j - x_j^{\min}}{x_j^{\max} - x_j^{\min}} \, (N_j - 1)
\end{equation}
its fractional grid index, where $N_j$ is the number of nodes of dimension $j$. For each vertex $\mathbf{v} \in \{0, 1\}^D$ of the enclosing cell, the barycentric weight is
\begin{equation}
    W_{\mathbf{v}} = \prod_{j=1}^{D} \left[ v_j \cdot \delta_j + (1 - v_j)(1 - \delta_j) \right],
    \qquad \delta_j = n_j - \lfloor n_j \rfloor,
    \label{baricentric_eq}
\end{equation}
so that all threads in a warp execute the same instruction stream regardless of where their states land. In what follows, $\mathcal{V}(s')$ denotes the set of the $2^D$ vertices of the cell enclosing $s'$, so the interpolated value of a next state reads $\sum_{\mathbf{v} \in \mathcal{V}(s')} W_{\mathbf{v}}\, V(\mathbf{v})$.

\subsection{Interpolation Preserves the Convergence Guarantee}
\label{sec:guarantee}
Discretizing the state space forces the value function to be evaluated between grid nodes, and the choice of interpolator determines whether the discretized backup keeps the convergence properties of the exact one. The scheme above is the barycentric framework of Munos and Moore \cite{Munos1998}: because the weights of Eq.~\eqref{baricentric_eq} are non-negative and sum to one, the interpolation operator $\mathcal{I}$ is \emph{non-expansive} in the supremum norm,
\begin{equation*}
    \|\mathcal{I}V - \mathcal{I}W\|_\infty \le \|V - W\|_\infty ,
\end{equation*}
so the interpolated backup inherits the contraction structure of the exact operator instead of amplifying approximation error, and the discretized problem is consistent with the continuous one as the mesh is refined \cite{Munos1998, Munos2002}.

This is the point of the architecture. Integrating the dynamics inside the kernel does not modify the mathematical operator at all: each thread computes the same next state, the same $2^D$ vertices, and the same convex weights that a tabular implementation would have precomputed, so the $\mathcal{O}(N_s)$ footprint costs nothing in the mathematics. The complexity reduction and the preservation of the guarantee are therefore obtained together, and it is the combination that matters: the guarantee is what makes the computed policy a trustworthy optimum, and the reduced footprint is what lets the grid that carries the guarantee be fine enough to resolve the recovery.

Two properties of the present formulation should be stated explicitly, since they determine what the converged solution certifies. First, the recursion is \emph{undiscounted} (unit discount factor): the objective is the total altitude change to absorption, and no discount factor is introduced, so the operator is non-expansive rather than a strict contraction. Convergence rests instead on the stochastic-shortest-path structure of the problem \cite{Bertsekas2017}, and the discretized process belongs to that class exactly, not approximately: the barycentric weights, non-negative and summing to one, are formally transition probabilities, so the interpolated deterministic dynamics define a genuine stochastic shortest path over the grid nodes \cite{Munos1998}. Its hypotheses are met:
\begin{itemize}
    \item The level-flight set $\{\gamma \ge 0\}$ and the departure set are absorbing and cost-free thereafter, so the value on both is known exactly.
    \item Every state admits at least one policy that reaches one of them.
    \item The stage reward is non-positive throughout the descent, so a policy that never reaches either terminal set accumulates unbounded loss and is excluded by the maximization.
\end{itemize}
Second, the object that converges is the optimal value function of the \emph{discretized, interpolated, finite-action} Markov decision process induced by the grid, the action set and the control interval. Optimality is exact for that process; its distance to the continuous optimum is a discretization question, addressed for Case~I in Sec.~\ref{sec:scaling} by refinement.

\subsection{The Control Interval $\Delta t$}
\label{sec:delta_t}
The integration interval $\Delta t$ appearing in
Algorithm~\ref{alg:parallel-policy-iteration} is a first-class solver
parameter rather than a fixed constant of the method, because its value
couples directly to the spatial grid through the barycentric backup.
If $\Delta t$ is chosen so small that the RK4 displacement covers only
a small fraction of a grid cell, the interpolation weights of
Eq.~\eqref{baricentric_eq} concentrate on the origin vertex: the state
effectively transitions to itself, value information propagates only a
fraction of a cell per sweep, and near-ties between actions are
amplified in the $\arg\max$. If $\Delta t$ is too large, the one-step
transition skips over cells, degrading the consistency of the
multilinear backup and the detection of terminal-set crossings. The
natural scale is a Courant--Friedrichs--Lewy (CFL) type condition
\cite{Courant1967}: the displacement per backup
should be commensurate with one cell,
$\Delta t^\ast \approx \bigl(\sum_i |\dot{x}_i| / h_i\bigr)^{-1}$,
where $h_i$ is the grid spacing of dimension $i$.

The kernel accordingly decouples the two roles the interval plays.
The \emph{control} interval, over which the action is held and the
Bellman backup is applied, is a configurable parameter, selected per
case study against the CFL-like scale above and reported with each
discretization; internally it is subdivided into RK4
\emph{micro-steps}, so that integration accuracy and terminal-event
detection (success and crash crossings are checked at micro-step
resolution) are independent of the backup interval. The solver
additionally supports a state-endogenous variant that evaluates
$\Delta t(s) = \min\{\Delta t_{max},\, (\sum_i |\dot{x}_i(s)| /
h_i)^{-1}\}$ on the fly inside the kernel, at no memory cost, since
the derivatives are already computed for the RK4 stage; the case
studies reported here use the fixed-interval configuration.

\subsection{Policy Iteration}
Where prior work in this domain used Value Iteration \cite{Bunge2018}, this solver implements Policy Iteration, summarized in Algorithm~\ref{alg:parallel-policy-iteration}. Policy Iteration requires a full policy-evaluation step but typically converges in far fewer iterations, moving between vertices of the policy polytope rather than approaching the fixed point linearly \cite{Bertsekas2017}. The evaluation step, traditionally the bottleneck of the method, is here an iterative sweep over the entire state array in a single kernel launch per pass, so its cost is the per-sweep cost already profiled in Table~\ref{tab:profiling_L4}. One safeguard deserves note: the evaluation is run to a tolerance rather than to exactness, and the value function is initialized at zero, an upper bound under non-positive rewards. The scheme is therefore a modified policy iteration \cite{Bertsekas2017}, whose convergence under the conditions of Sec.~\ref{sec:guarantee} does not require the initial random policy to be proper; a policy that traps part of the state space merely sinks the value there sweep by sweep until the next improvement replaces it.

\begin{algorithm}[H]
\caption{Massively Parallel Policy Iteration for High-Fidelity Grids}
\label{alg:parallel-policy-iteration}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\STATE \textbf{Initialize:} $V(s) \leftarrow 0$ and $\pi(s) \leftarrow \text{random}$ for all $s \in \mathcal{S}$, the set of the $N_s$ grid states
\STATE $policy\_stable \leftarrow \text{false}$
\WHILE{\NOT $policy\_stable$}
    \STATE \COMMENT{Step 1: Parallel Policy Evaluation}
    \REPEAT
        \STATE $\Delta \leftarrow 0$
        \FORALL{thread $s \in \mathcal{S}$ \textbf{in parallel}}
            \STATE $V_{\text{old}} \leftarrow V(s)$
            \STATE $s' \leftarrow \text{RK4}(s, \pi(s), \Delta t)$
            \STATE $V(s) \leftarrow g(s, \pi(s)) + \sum_{\mathbf{v} \in \mathcal{V}(s')} W_{\mathbf{v}}\, V(\mathbf{v})$ \COMMENT{$\mathcal{V}(s')$: vertices of the cell containing $s'$; weights from Eq.~\eqref{baricentric_eq}}
            \STATE $\Delta \leftarrow \max(\Delta, |V_{\text{old}} - V(s)|)$
        \ENDFOR
    \UNTIL{$\Delta < \text{tolerance}$}

    \STATE \COMMENT{Step 2: Parallel Policy Improvement}
    \STATE $policy\_stable \leftarrow \text{true}$
    \FORALL{thread $s \in \mathcal{S}$ \textbf{in parallel}}
        \STATE $old\_action \leftarrow \pi(s)$
        \STATE $\pi(s) \leftarrow \arg\max_{a \in A} \left[ g(s, a) + \sum_{\mathbf{v} \in \mathcal{V}(s')} W_{\mathbf{v}}\, V(\mathbf{v}) \right]$, with $s' = \text{RK4}(s, a, \Delta t)$
        \IF{$old\_action \neq \pi(s)$}
            \STATE $policy\_stable \leftarrow \text{false}$
        \ENDIF
    \ENDFOR
\ENDWHILE
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

\section{Problem Formulation}

The minimum-altitude-loss pullout is naturally posed as a free-final-time
optimal control problem with a terminal constraint,
Eq.~\eqref{eq:ocp_free_tf}: find the control history that steers the
aircraft from the upset state $x_0$ to level flight,
$\gamma(t_f) = 0$, while minimizing the altitude lost along the way.
Problems of this form can be solved by indirect methods based on the
calculus of variations, or by direct methods such as collocation-based
trajectory optimization. Either way, the solution is an open-loop control
history valid for a single initial condition: recovering from a different
upset requires solving the problem anew.

\begin{equation}
    \label{eq:ocp_free_tf}
    h^*(x_0) \;=\; \min_{a(\cdot),\; t_f} \int_0^{t_f} -V \sin\gamma \,\mathrm{d}t
    \qquad
    \text{s.t.}
    \quad
    \begin{aligned}
        \dot{x} &= f(x, a),\text{ } x(0)= x_0 \text{ and } \gamma(t_f) = 0
    \end{aligned}
\end{equation}

Two properties of Eq.~\eqref{eq:ocp_free_tf} permit a stronger result. The
running cost $-V\sin\gamma$ depends only on the state, and it vanishes on
the terminal manifold $\gamma = 0$. Consequently, if the level-flight set
$\{\gamma \ge 0\}$ is made absorbing (freezing the dynamics once the
flight-path angle reaches zero), the trajectory accumulates no further cost
after recovery, and the problem becomes the infinite-horizon formulation of
Eq.~\eqref{eq:ocp_inf_horizon}. Since $\gamma(t)$ is continuous, the
absorbing set is entered exactly at $\gamma = 0$, where the running cost
vanishes, so the integral remains finite.

\begin{equation}
    \label{eq:ocp_inf_horizon}
    h^*(x_0) \;=\; \min_{a(\cdot)} \int_0^{\infty} -V \sin\gamma \,\mathrm{d}t
    \qquad
    \text{s.t.}
    \quad
    \begin{aligned}
        \dot{x} &=
            \begin{cases}
                f(x, a), & \gamma < 0 \\
                0,       & \gamma \ge 0
            \end{cases} \\
        x(0) &= x_0
    \end{aligned}
\end{equation}

The value function of Eq.~\eqref{eq:ocp_inf_horizon} satisfies a stationary
Bellman equation, which dynamic programming solves for all initial
conditions simultaneously: the solution is no longer a single trajectory
but the optimal feedback policy $a^* = \pi^*(x)$, yielding the
minimum-altitude-loss recovery from every state in the flight envelope.
The solver implements this minimization as the equivalent maximization
of a non-positive stage reward: $g(s,a)$ in
Algorithm~\ref{alg:parallel-policy-iteration} accumulates the
(negative) altitude change over one control interval, the negative of
the running cost of Eq.~\eqref{eq:ocp_inf_horizon}, so the $\arg\max$
of the backup and the minimization of Eq.~\eqref{eq:ocp_inf_horizon}
select the same actions.



\section{Case I: 3-DOF Banked Pullout Validation against Established Literature}
\label{sec:case1}

\subsection{Reduced-Order Equations of Motion}

Following Bunge et al.\ \cite{Bunge2018}, the banked pullout is
modeled as a three-state point mass under four simplifying
assumptions: the lift coefficient and the bank rate are tracked by
high-bandwidth inner loops (elevator and aileron, respectively) whose
dynamics are neglected; sideslip remains zero; drag depends on the
state only through the lift coefficient; and power is at idle. The
state vector is $\mathbf{x} = [\gamma,\, V/V_s,\, \mu]^\top$
(flight path angle, normalized airspeed, and bank angle), and the
control vector collects the two commanded quantities,
$\mathbf{a} = [C_L^{cmd},\, \dot{\mu}_{cmd}]^\top$:
\begin{align}
    \dot{\gamma} &= \frac{\rho S}{2m}\, V\, C_L^{cmd} \cos\mu
                    - \frac{g \cos\gamma}{V},
    \label{eq:gam_3dof}\\
    \dot{V} &= -g \sin\gamma - \frac{\rho S}{2m}\, V^2\,
               C_D\!\left(C_L^{cmd}\right),
    \label{eq:vdot_3dof}\\
    \dot{\mu} &= \dot{\mu}_{cmd}.
    \label{eq:mudot_3dof}
\end{align}
Equations~\eqref{eq:gam_3dof}--\eqref{eq:vdot_3dof} are written in
the dimensional airspeed $V$; the solver stores and integrates the
normalized state component $V/V_s$, whose rate is $\dot{V}/V_s$.
The drag coefficient is a static function of the commanded lift,
obtained by inverting the linear lift curve
$\alpha = (C_L - C_{L_0})/C_{L_\alpha}$ and evaluating the quadratic
polar $C_D = C_{D_0} + C_{D_\alpha}\alpha + C_{D_{\alpha^2}}\alpha^2$
($C_{L_0} = 0.41$, $C_{L_\alpha} = 4.70$\,rad$^{-1}$,
$C_{D_0} = 0.0525$, $C_{D_\alpha} = 0.2068$,
$C_{D_{\alpha^2}} = 1.8712$). The aircraft is the same Grumman
American AA-1 Yankee of Case II, at the flight weight used by
\cite{Bunge2018} ($m = 697.18$\,kg, $S = 9.1147$\,m$^2$).

The control bounds encode the physics that the reduced model does not
resolve. The commanded lift is limited to
$C_L^{cmd} \in [-0.5,\, 1.0]$, a margin of $0.2$ below the positive
stall value ($C_{L} = 1.2$) and above the negative (inverted) stall
value ($C_L \approx -0.7$), so that commanded-lift trajectories never
enter the separated-flow regime the model cannot represent. The bank
rate is limited to $|\dot{\mu}_{cmd}| \leq 30$\,deg/s, the
steady-state roll rate with full aileron at stall speed
\cite{Bunge2018}. This commanded-$C_L$, commanded-bank-rate
abstraction is precisely the perfect-inner-loop assumption that
Case II removes by resolving the pitch dynamics explicitly.

\subsection{Baseline Discretization Benchmarking}
For validation against the published benchmark, the state-control space was first discretized on the rectangular grid of Bunge et al.\ \cite{Bunge2018}. The flight dynamics are integrated with a fixed time step of $\Delta t = 0.1$ seconds. The stage reward $g(s, a)$ is the altitude change over the interval, non-positive during the descent, minus a quadratic penalty that ensures the smoothness of the bank rate command policy, preventing jagged control surfaces: the smoothing device introduced by Bunge et al.\ \cite{Bunge2018}, here with a lighter weight ($0.001$ vs.\ their $0.01$) that preserves the regularizing role while keeping the altitude-loss term strictly dominant:

\begin{equation}
    g(s, a) = V \sin \gamma \, \Delta t - 0.001 \dot{\mu}_{cmd}^2
    \label{eq:reward-3dof}
\end{equation}

The baseline grid boundaries and increments, identical to those of
Bunge et al., are reproduced in Table~\ref{tab:discretization}
(Appendix~\ref{app:case1_baseline}); Sec.~\ref{sec:scaling} refines
them.

On this baseline grid the full Policy Iteration solve is essentially
instantaneous: Table~\ref{tab:profiling} (Appendix~\ref{app:case1_baseline})
breaks down the wall-clock cost as measured with CUDA events on an
NVIDIA GeForce RTX 4090. The
profile is the compute-bound architecture of Section~II made visible.
Policy evaluation (the phase that traditional implementations
amortize with precomputed transition tables) accounts for
$98.5\%$ of the runtime and executes entirely on the GPU at
$12$\,ms per sweep; policy improvement, which evaluates all $91$
actions per state, costs $0.16$\,ms per iteration; and the total
device-to-host traffic is $0.2$\,ms. Solving the benchmark problem of
\cite{Bunge2018} to full convergence takes $0.38$\,s end to end.

\subsection{Baseline Validation and Optimal Value Function Topology}
Upon convergence on the baseline grid, the optimal value function $V^*(s)$ maps each state to the minimal altitude loss achievable from it. Because the stage reward $g(s, a)$ is defined primarily by the incremental altitude change, the total altitude loss $\Delta h_{min}$ required to return the aircraft to level flight ($\gamma = 0^\circ$) can be extracted directly from the converged grid:

\begin{equation}
    \Delta h_{min}(V_0, \gamma_0, \mu_0) \approx -V^*(V_0, \gamma_0, \mu_0)
\end{equation}
where the approximation absorbs the small accumulated contribution of the bank-rate penalty of Eq.~\eqref{eq:reward-3dof}. The converged value function was queried across slices of constant initial normalized airspeed ($V_0/V_s \in \{1.2, 2.0, 3.0, 4.0\}$). The resulting altitude-loss contours over the initial bank and flight-path envelope (Fig.~\ref{fig:combined_contours}, Appendix~\ref{app:case1_baseline}) reproduce the global topology published by Bunge et al.\ \cite{Bunge2018}, validating the accuracy of the on-the-fly RK4 integration and the consistency of the barycentric interpolator.

\subsection{Trajectory-Level Validation Against Direct Optimal Control}

A second, independent check quantifies the solver against
continuous-time optimal control: for each initial condition of
Figures~3 and~4 of \cite{Bunge2018} (the two sweeps of
Table~\ref{tab:casadi_benchmark}), the free-final-time
problem of Eq.~\eqref{eq:ocp_free_tf} was solved by direct
collocation \cite{Betts1998} (CasADi \cite{Andersson2019} with IPOPT
\cite{Wachter2006}), warm-started with the DP rollout, since the
nonconvex NLP needs an initialization in the correct basin. Their
agreement is a bidirectional check: the NLP confirms the DP's local
optimality, and the DP certifies that the NLP descended the global
basin. The two objects also differ in kind: the NLP decides one
open-loop control history per entry, all node controls jointly,
re-solved for each initial condition; the DP is a feedback law over
the entire envelope, optimal again from any perturbed state.

Table~\ref{tab:casadi_benchmark} gives the number on the refined
grid of Sec.~\ref{sec:scaling}; the trajectory pairs are reproduced
in Fig.~\ref{fig:casadi_trajectories},
Appendix~\ref{app:case1_baseline}. The metric, the gap over the
deepest loss of each sweep, is at most $1.4\%$ ($0.9\%$ on average,
$2.1$\,m at worst); the qualitative structure of \cite{Bunge2018} is
reproduced, including the inverted pullout at the near-inverted
entry. The continuous solution is the
better of the two at every entry, as it must be: every discretized
policy is a feasible solution of the continuous problem, so its
optimum can only match or beat the DP; a reversed sign would indict
the NLP's convergence, not the method.

The gap splits into an accounting artifact and a structural price.
The artifact: the evaluation rollout accumulates altitude with
forward Euler at $\Delta t = 0.1$\,s, a first-order, one-signed
error (the NLP's collocation error is orders smaller) that
telescopes to $(\Delta t/2)\, V_0\, |\sin\gamma_0|$
and matches the smallest gap of each sweep to three figures
($0.96$\,m at $\gamma_0 = -30^\circ$, $1.66$\,m at $-60^\circ$). The
structural price, at most $1.2$\,m: the NLP, free to pick any
control value at any instant, simply selects the optimal one, while
the discrete policy can realize an intermediate control only by
alternating its $91$ actions at the $0.1$\,s hold; since the optimum
is nearly bang-bang, both fly the same extremes for most of the
maneuver and the price concentrates at the switch times.
Tightening the NLP would lower its own losses and widen the gap, not
close it.

\section{Case II: 4-DOF Symmetric Stall Recovery with Riley (1985) Aerodynamics}

This section poses the question the paper was built to answer: flown
optimally, does the symmetric stall recovery apply power at once or
delay it, and what does either choice cost? The 3-DOF pullout of
Sec.~\ref{sec:case1} validated the solver but could not ask this:
its controls were the outputs of an assumed-perfect inner loop
(commanded lift and bank rate), and it had no throttle. Here the
framework is applied to a four-state longitudinal model that
resolves what the pilot actually commands. The state vector is
$\mathbf{x} = [\gamma,\, V/V_s,\, \alpha,\, q]^\top$
(flight path angle, normalized airspeed, angle of attack, and pitch
rate) and the control vector is
$\mathbf{u} = [\delta_e,\, \delta_t]^\top$
(elevator deflection and throttle position): the pitch dynamics are
resolved rather than assumed tracked, and the timing of power
application becomes a decision variable of the optimization instead
of an input from doctrine. The aerodynamics come from Riley's
propulsion-coupled wind-tunnel tables, measured through stall and
post-stall at two propulsive conditions, and the propulsion carries
his first-order engine lag; both are developed in the following
subsections. The physical parameters
correspond to the Grumman American AA-1 Yankee as tabulated by Riley
\cite{Riley1985} and are collected in Table~\ref{tab:aa1_params} of
Appendix~\ref{app:riley_tables}, together with the derived reference
quantities used throughout this section, in particular the stall
speed $V_s \approx 31.6$\,m/s that normalizes the airspeed state.

The model is deliberately symmetric: wings level, zero sideslip, no
lateral--directional states. Real stalls frequently depart
asymmetrically (a wing drop at the break is the precursor of the
incipient spin), and no longitudinal model can speak to that phase.
The scope of this section is therefore the symmetric stall proper:
the regime in which the certified procedures disagree on power
timing, and the one the flight-test comparison of Gratton et al.\
\cite{Gratton2014} addressed.

\subsection{Equations of Motion}

The longitudinal dynamics are
\begin{align}
    \dot{\gamma} &= \frac{L}{mV} - \frac{g\cos\gamma}{V}, \label{eq:gam_riley}\\
    \dot{V}      &= -g\sin\gamma - \frac{D}{m}, \label{eq:vdot_riley}\\
    \dot{\alpha} &= q - \dot{\gamma}, \label{eq:alpha_dot_riley}\\
    \dot{q}      &= \frac{M_y}{I_{yy}} = \frac{\bar{q}Sc\,C_m}{I_{yy}},
    \label{eq:q_dot_riley}
\end{align}
with $L = \bar{q}S\,C_L$, $D = \bar{q}S\,C_D$,
$\bar{q} = \tfrac{1}{2}\rho V^2$, and the dimensionless pitch rate
$\hat{q} = qc/(2V)$ entering the aerodynamic coefficients below. As
in Sec.~\ref{sec:case1}, the equations are written in the
dimensional airspeed $V$ while the solver stores and integrates the
normalized $V/V_s$.

Notably, Eqs.~\eqref{eq:gam_riley}--\eqref{eq:vdot_riley} contain
\emph{no explicit thrust terms} ($T\sin\alpha$, $T\cos\alpha$). This is
not an approximation but a direct consequence of how Riley
\cite{Riley1985} defines the power-on aerodynamic data. The wind-tunnel
measurements at the powered condition were conducted with a running
propeller installed on the airframe, so the force balance measured the
\textit{net axial force} acting on the complete propulsion--airframe
system. Normalized by $\bar{q}S$, the tabulated drag entry is therefore
a net axial-force coefficient,
\begin{equation}
    C_{D_o}\bigl|_{C_T=0.5} = \frac{D_\text{aero} - T}{\bar{q}S}
    = C_{D,\text{aero}} - C_T,
    \label{eq:cd_net}
\end{equation}
where the thrust coefficient is defined as
\begin{equation}
    C_T = \frac{T}{\bar{q}\, S}.
    \label{eq:ct_def}
\end{equation}
The negative values of the power-on drag column at low angles of attack
(Table~\ref{tab:riley_cd}: $C_{D_o} = -0.347$ at $\alpha = 0^\circ$)
provide direct empirical confirmation: aerodynamic drag is intrinsically
positive, so a negative tabulated value can only arise when the
propeller thrust dominates, yielding a net forward force. Inserting an
explicit thrust term into Eq.~\eqref{eq:vdot_riley} while also using the
Riley power-on tables would therefore \textit{double-count} the
propulsive contribution. Thrust enters the model exclusively through
$C_T$, which selects the operating point of the aerodynamic tables.
The thrust itself follows Riley's Appendix~A: the cockpit throttle $\delta_t$
maps to the intermediate setting
$\delta_t' = 0.65\,\delta_t + 0.35$ of his Eq.~(A3), and sea-level
thrust is interpolated from the twelve-entry table of his Eq.~(A9),
$T = T_0(\delta_t') + T_1(\delta_t')\,V$: about $2.0$\,kN at full
throttle statically, decaying with airspeed.
Definition~\eqref{eq:ct_def} couples the
propulsive and aerodynamic states: as the aircraft decelerates during
the stall, $\bar{q}$ collapses and $C_T$ rises for a fixed throttle,
shifting the interpolated coefficients toward the power-on tables.

One element of the propulsion model is deliberately left out of the
dynamic program: Riley's Eq.~(A4) places a first-order lag,
$\tau_e\, \dot{\delta}_t = \delta_{t,c} - \delta_t$, between the
commanded and the effective throttle. Riley defines $\tau_e$ and
never tabulates its value; digitizing his published throttle
transients and fitting the lag through his Eqs.~(A3) and~(A12)
recovers $\tau_e = 0.8 \pm 0.2$\,s, and $\tau_e = 0.85$\,s is used
throughout. Carrying the lag into the dynamic program would promote
the effective throttle to a fifth state and multiply the grid:
eleven bins take the $14.9$ million states of
Sec.~\ref{sec:4dof_solver} to $164$ million, and the solve from
hours to days. The policy is therefore solved against an ideal
engine, and every trajectory in this section is flown through the
lagged one; Sec.~\ref{sec:time_domain} quantifies what the lag
costs and the sensitivity of the conclusions to $\tau_e$.

\subsection{Propulsion-Coupled Aerodynamic Model from Wind-Tunnel Data}
\label{sec:riley_aero}

The aerodynamic model replaces the constant-derivative formulations
of prior stall-recovery studies \cite{Grillo2023} with the complete
tabulated aerodynamics of Riley \cite{Riley1985} (NASA TM-86309):
not a fitted model but measurements, from full-scale powered
wind-tunnel tests of the aircraft type in the Langley $30$- by
$60$-Foot Tunnel with a thrust-torque balance between engine shaft
and propeller. Seven coefficient tables are extracted directly from
his Table III: the baseline lift, axial-force, and pitching-moment
coefficients ($C_{L_o}$, $C_{D_o}$, $C_{m_o}$), the pitch-rate
derivatives ($C_{L_{\hat{q}}}$, $C_{m_{\hat{q}}}$), and the elevator
effectiveness derivatives ($C_{L_{\delta_e}}$,
$C_{m_{\delta_e}}$), each on a common 14-point angle-of-attack grid
from $-10^\circ$ to $40^\circ$, at power-off ($C_T = 0$) and a
representative power-on condition ($C_T = 0.5$). The complete
transcribed tables are collected in
Appendix~\ref{app:riley_tables},
Fig.~\ref{fig:riley_coeffs} plots the four that drive the recovery
physics.

\begin{figure}[H]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{img/riley_coeff_row.pdf}
    \caption{The Riley (1985) coefficients that drive the recovery,
    at the two tabulated propulsive conditions (complete tables in
    Appendix~\ref{app:riley_tables}). (a)~Baseline lift and net
    axial force: the power-off flat-top stall plateau against the
    monotone power-on growth of slipstream augmentation, and the

    \label{fig:riley_coeffs}

Three features of the data drive the results of this section, and
none survives a constant-derivative approximation. First, the
power-off lift curve shows the characteristic flat-top plateau
($C_{L_o} = 1.26$ between $14^\circ$ and $18^\circ$) while the
power-on curve keeps \emph{increasing} up to $40^\circ$: the
propeller slipstream raises the dynamic pressure over the wing, so
powered lift remains available deep into the post-stall regime. The
plateau onset defines the reference stall angle
$\alpha_s = 14^\circ$; it is a power-off boundary, and the policy
will be seen to exploit the powered lift beyond it. Throughout this
paper, \emph{stall boundary} refers to this power-off angle: under
power the aerodynamic stall itself moves, but $\alpha_s$ remains the
reference the recovery is measured against. Second, the
power-on axial-force column is negative below
$\alpha \approx 18^\circ$: the empirical fingerprint of the embedded
thrust of Eq.~\eqref{eq:cd_net}. Third, the pitch-rate damping
$C_{m_{\hat{q}}}$ more than doubles across the stall, from $-7.15$
at cruise to $-15.4$ power-off ($-20.3$ power-on).

The slipstream increment is aerodynamic, not mechanical: an explicit
thrust term projects a force but leaves the coefficients untouched,
so decoupled models systematically underpredict the normal force
available during a powered recovery. Altitude-loss figures published
on such models \cite{Grillo2023} are therefore not commensurable
with the results below, and no quantitative comparison is attempted;
the validation of the framework rests on Case~I, where benchmark and
solver share the same plant.

Each coefficient is evaluated bilinearly: a linear table lookup
along the $\alpha$ axis at each of the two tabulated $C_T$ levels,
blended linearly in $C_T$, with the lookup clipped to the tabulated
range $C_T \in [0, 0.5]$. The clip applies to the table lookup only,
not to the thrust itself: in the recovery regime $C_T$ routinely
exceeds the tabulated $0.5$ (full throttle at $0.57\,V_s$ gives
$C_T \approx 1$), and the excess axial force is restored through
Riley's Appendix~B correction,
$\Delta C_{D_T} = -0.80\,(C_T - 0.5)\cos\alpha$ for $C_T > 0.5$ and
$-0.80\,C_T\cos\alpha$ for $C_T < 0$, identically zero inside the
tabulated range. The lift and moment columns, by contrast, saturate
at the $C_T = 0.5$ measurement: the slipstream augmentation beyond
the tabulated condition is not extrapolated, a conservative choice.
The complete aerodynamic model is then
\begin{align}
    C_L &= C_{L_o}(\alpha, C_T)
         + C_{L_{\delta_e}}(\alpha, C_T)\,\delta_e
         + C_{L_{\hat{q}}}(\alpha, C_T)\,\hat{q}
         + C_{L_{\hat{\dot{\alpha}}}}(\alpha, C_T)\,\hat{\dot{\alpha}},
    \label{eq:cl_riley}\\
    C_D &= C_{D_o}(\alpha, C_T)
         + C_{D_{\delta_e}}(\alpha, C_T)\,\delta_e
         + C_{D_{\delta_e^2}}(\alpha, C_T)\,\delta_e^2,
    \label{eq:cd_riley}\\
    C_m &= C_{m_o}(\alpha, C_T)
         + C_{m_{\delta_e}}(\alpha, C_T)\,\delta_e
         + C_{m_{\hat{q}}}(\alpha, C_T)\,\hat{q}
         + C_{m_{\hat{\dot{\alpha}}}}(\alpha, C_T)\,\hat{\dot{\alpha}}
         + \frac{\Delta x_{cg}}{\bar{c}}\, C_L,
    \label{eq:cm_riley}
\end{align}
with $\hat{\dot{\alpha}} = \dot{\alpha}\,c/(2V)$ the dimensionless rate
of change of angle of attack, formed like $\hat{q}$.

Every longitudinal coefficient tabulated by Riley is used. The elevator
drag terms $C_{D_{\delta_e}}$ and $C_{D_{\delta_e^2}}$ matter because the
elevator is deflected throughout a recovery, and the quadratic term
gives the nose-down phase an interior optimum rather than an
actuator-limited one. The rate derivatives
$C_{L_{\hat{\dot{\alpha}}}}$ and $C_{m_{\hat{\dot{\alpha}}}}$ are
reported separately from $C_{\hat{q}}$ by Riley --- many models lump the
two into a single damping term --- and both \emph{change sign at the
stall}: $C_{m_{\hat{\dot{\alpha}}}}$ goes from $-0.38$ at
$\alpha = 12^\circ$ to $+1.80$ at $14^\circ$, so it damps below the
stall and anti-damps above it. Omitting them is therefore not uniform
over the domain this maneuver traverses.

Because $C_L$ depends on $\dot{\alpha}$ while
Eq.~\eqref{eq:alpha_dot_riley} makes $\dot{\alpha}$ depend on
$\dot{\gamma}$, which Eq.~\eqref{eq:gam_riley} in turn obtains from
$C_L$, the rate terms close an implicit loop. It is resolved
algebraically in closed form rather than by fixed-point iteration,
so that the CUDA kernel and the CPU reference evaluate the identical
expression; the correction is small, the implicit denominator
staying within $1 \pm 0.005$ over the whole speed range of the grid.

The last term of Eq.~\eqref{eq:cm_riley} transfers the pitching
moment to a center of gravity displaced $\Delta x_{cg}$ aft of the
Riley reference ($0.25\,\bar{c}$); $\Delta x_{cg} = 0$ is used
throughout, and the robustness study of Sec.~\ref{sec:robustness}
exercises the term. All lookups execute inside the CUDA kernel,
keeping the solver compute-bound.

\subsection{Discretization, Stage Cost, and Solver Configuration}
\label{sec:4dof_solver}

The stage reward retains the
pure altitude-loss structure of the infinite-horizon formulation,
\begin{equation}
    g(s, a) = V \sin\gamma \,\Delta t,
    \label{eq:reward_4dof}
\end{equation}
stated here in reward (maximization) form, the negated altitude term
of the Case I cost, with the absorbing success set
$\{\gamma \geq 0\}$. Two deliberate differences from the
Case I objective of Eq.~\eqref{eq:reward-3dof} follow from the
higher model fidelity. First, the quadratic bank-rate smoothing
penalty inherited from \cite{Bunge2018} has no counterpart here. In
the reduced-order model the bank rate is a free kinematic input, so
infinitely many roll histories tie and the penalty stands in for
the unmodeled actuation. Here the actuation \emph{is} modeled: the
elevator acts through the moment chain
$\delta_e \to C_m \to \dot{q} \to q \to \dot{\alpha}$, whose inertia
and stall-amplified pitch damping (Fig.~\ref{fig:riley_coeffs}c)
low-pass filter the command. Residual action ties on the constraint
arc still produce bang-bang switching in the raw policy map (the
discrete approximation of the singular-arc equivalent control,
Sec.~\ref{sec:time_domain}), but the plant dynamics absorb
it: $\alpha(t)$ rides the stall boundary smoothly, and
the closed-loop altitude loss is insensitive to command smoothing
within $0.3$\,m. The objective remains pure altitude loss. Second,
unlike Case~I, whose commanded lift is bounded within safe limits,
the 4-DOF model resolves the post-stall aerodynamics and can
genuinely crash. The catastrophic terminal set
$\{|\alpha| \geq 40^\circ\} \cup \{\gamma \leq -\pi + 0.05\}$
(angle-of-attack departure beyond the wind-tunnel data, or a
near-vertical inverted dive) carries a fixed penalty of
$-1000\,V_s$. Its $\alpha = +40^\circ$ branch is the upper edge of
the grid, where the penalty is pre-assigned to the terminal cells
and propagates inward through the multilinear backup; the negative
branch and the inverted dive lie outside the grid and are detected
during integration.

Table~\ref{tab:4dof_discretization} details the discretization of
the state-action space: $56 \times 81 \times 80 \times 41 =
14{,}878{,}080$ states by $451$ actions.

Every closed-loop evaluation reported in this
section executes the policy through the barycentric action blend: at
an off-grid state, the commanded action is the convex combination of
the argmax actions of the $16$ surrounding vertices under the weights
of Eq.~\eqref{baricentric_eq}. Section~\ref{sec:time_domain},
where the closed-loop trajectory is flown and the policy's
switching surface becomes visible, shows why the blend is the
solution concept the converged policy requires and not a
smoothing: on that surface the blend realizes the Filippov
solution \cite{Filippov1988}.
Evaluated greedily instead (re-deriving the $\arg\max$ at the
continuous state, which discards the combination), the policy
chatters between the bang-bang extremes and the pitch-rate
overshoot costs several times the altitude.

Each Bellman transition integrates the dynamics
over a control interval of $\Delta t = 0.01$\,s subdivided into ten
RK4 micro-steps of $1$\,ms, executed entirely within GPU registers;
success and crash crossings are detected at micro-step resolution,
so intra-interval boundary events are never skipped. The 4-DOF
formulation uses the 16-vertex ($2^4$) instance of the barycentric
interpolation of Eq.~\eqref{baricentric_eq}.

Two further conventions govern every closed-loop number reported in
this section. First, all shaping weights available in the solver
(pitch-rate penalty, angle-of-attack barrier, control-effort and
throttle terms) are zero; the only non-altitude term in the
objective is the crash penalty. Neither full throttle nor the
switch near $\alpha_s$ in Sec.~\ref{sec:policy_structure} is
incentivized by a weight.

Second, the altitude loss of a rollout is measured by a
\emph{dive-and-return} rule.\label{sec:metric} The episode is integrated at the control
interval from the stated entry; a dive is registered once
$\gamma < -0.5^\circ$, and the recovery is declared complete when the
flight path returns to level, either by crossing $\gamma \ge 0$ or by
converging to it --- $\gamma$ within $0.5^\circ$ of level with
$|\dot{\gamma}| < 0.05^\circ$/s sustained for $0.5$\,s. The second
clause is needed because near $V_0 = V_s$ the optimum approaches level
flight asymptotically and never crosses; genuine crossings arrive at
$0.5$--$1.2^\circ$/s, three orders of magnitude above the tolerance,
so the clause never fires on a crossing trajectory. Episodes are
truncated at $15$\,s; truncated entries are marked wherever they are
tabulated, and their altitude loss is a lower bound rather than a
completed recovery.

\subsection{Optimal Policy: Full-Power Structure and the Stall-Boundary Switching Surface}
\label{sec:policy_structure}

Figure~\ref{fig:4dof_policy_riley} presents the converged solution
over the $(\alpha, \gamma)$ plane at the $q = 0$ slice, one row per
airspeed $V_0/V_s \in \{0.9, 1.0, 1.1\}$: the exact DP policies for
commanded elevator ($\delta_e^*$) and throttle ($\delta_t^*$), and
the value function read as minimum altitude loss to recovery. In the
elevator maps dark blue is full nose-up, yellow full nose-down, and
the white curve traces the switching boundary, with the dashed
line at its mean angle of attack. The surface lives in
four dimensions, and Fig.~\ref{fig:policy_q_slices} sweeps
the pitch rate: with nose-down rotation in hand the boundary shifts
higher, about $2^\circ$ per $10$\,deg/s, with nose-up it drops.

Two structural features stand out. First, the throttle policy
saturates at full power essentially everywhere in the envelope of the
figure. Second, the elevator policy is bang-bang with a sharp
switching surface in the neighbourhood of the stall boundary:
closed-loop trajectories initiated across the deep-stall envelope
command full nose-down deflection ($\delta_e = +15^\circ$) until the
angle of attack falls through that surface, at which point the policy
switches to nose-up commands, regardless of how long that takes from
each initial condition.

The displacement of the boundary in Fig.~\ref{fig:policy_q_slices}
is the kinematics of the reversal made visible. Since $\dot{\alpha} = q - \dot{\gamma}$, a nose-down
pitch rate means the angle of attack is already falling on its own:
the rotation the push exists to build is already in hand, and
holding the deflection longer buys only elevator drag
(Eq.~\eqref{eq:cd_riley}). The optimum therefore releases the push
early, at higher angle of attack, and lets the rotation complete the
crossing; with nose-up rate working against it, the mirror argument
holds, and it keeps pushing to lower angles of attack before
reversing.

Table~\ref{tab:closed_loop_alpha} quantifies
both features on closed-loop trajectories, flown through the engine
lag across the stalled-entry envelope, and its two rows read very
differently. The commanded elevator reverses at angles of attack
that span $15.0$--$20.6^\circ$ across the entries, with a median of
$17.2^\circ$: \emph{when} to switch adapts to each recovery. The
arrested descent then rides at $\alpha = 13.9^\circ$ with a spread
of two tenths of a degree across every recovered entry:
\emph{where} to fly the pull does not.

\input{tables/table_closed_loop_alpha}

Each row has its physical explanation. In the arrest phase every
recovered simulation seeks the same angle of attack, around
$14^\circ$ (the arrest row of Table~\ref{tab:closed_loop_alpha}),
and Fig.~\ref{fig:alpha_trade} says why: it plots the marginal
trade encoded in the tables, lift gained against drag paid per
further degree of angle of attack, at both propulsive conditions.
Past ${\approx}14^\circ$ each additional degree buys less lift than
the drag it costs, with or without power, even though the power-on
lift keeps rising to $40^\circ$: there is a single best angle to
fly the pull, the crossing of that trade, and every recovery
converges to it. The flexible row is the mechanism of
Fig.~\ref{fig:policy_q_slices} at work: each trajectory arrives at
the reversal with its own pitch rate, and at the
$q \approx -20$\,deg/s they typically carry, the boundary sits near
$17^\circ$ at the shallow flight-path angles where the reversals
occur. The maneuver is thus adaptive where the state demands it,
the timing of the reversal, and rigid where the aerodynamics does,
the operating point of the pull; the switching criterion is in any
case a surface in state space, not a schedule in time. Together,
simultaneous full power and nose-down elevator, pull-up once the
wing unstalls, these are the structural fingerprint of the CAA
recovery sequence.

The nature of that statement deserves emphasis. Nothing in the
problem formulation refers to a recovery procedure: the reward is
the altitude increment, the dynamics are wind-tunnel tables, the
admissible controls are actuator limits. The CAA structure is read
directly off the converged solution of the Bellman equation: a
property of the optimum, not the winner of a comparison between
alternatives, and the sections that follow can corroborate it or
price departures from it, but cannot produce it or undo it. The
operational weight is carried by the second feature: the switch is
a surface in state space pinned to $\alpha_s$, not an instant on a
clock. A pilot cannot be instructed to rotate at a prescribed
instant from an entry whose onset they did not time, but can be
instructed to pull as the wing unstalls. That an optimizer free to
return any feedback law over a $14.9$-million-state grid returns
one expressible in the language of the certified procedure is the
substantive finding of this subsection.

\subsection{Flying the Optimum: Time-Domain Recovery and the
Monte Carlo Certificate}
\label{sec:time_domain}

The optimal policy of the preceding subsection is a field of
commands; this subsection flies it.
Figure~\ref{fig:4dof_trajectory_riley} shows the closed-loop
recovery from the canonical deep-stall entry (level flight,
$\gamma_0 = 0$; $\alpha_0 = 20^\circ$; $V_0 = 0.85\,V_s$;
$q_0 = 0$) under the exact DP policy, overlaid with the two
certified procedures flown from the same entry. Both procedures use
one scripted pilot: nose-down $\delta_e = +15^\circ$ held until the
unstall event, then a closed-loop pull that holds
$\alpha \approx 13^\circ$
($\delta_e = \mathrm{clip}(K_\alpha (\alpha - \alpha_{tgt}) + K_q q)$
with $K_\alpha = 10$, $K_q = 2$). Power ramps to full over $2$\,s
from $t = 0$ (CAA) or from the unstall event (FAA).
Pitch technique and pull authority are shared across the three
maneuvers by construction, so the trajectories differ only in when
power is applied.

The recovery unfolds in three phases. First, full nose-down
elevator and full power at once: the angle of attack falls from
$20^\circ$ to the stall boundary in $0.41$\,s, while the lagged
engine needs $1.95$\,s to deliver $90\%$ of the thrust commanded at
$t = 0$. Second, a \emph{sliding-mode} arc rides the boundary for
the remainder of the pullout ($\bar{\alpha} = 13.3^\circ$; the
arrest statistic of Table~\ref{tab:closed_loop_alpha}, averaged
only while the climb is being established, puts the same floor at
$13.9^\circ$). Third, the level-off. The chattering elevator of
panel~(e) is the bang-bang policy meeting its own mathematics.
The closed loop $\dot{x} = f(x, \pi(x))$ has a discontinuous
right-hand side (the elevator maps of
Fig.~\ref{fig:4dof_policy_riley}, full nose-up against full
nose-down across the white curve), and on the arc the
discontinuity attracts: the push above the boundary drives
$\alpha$ down, the pull below drives it up. Both fields point into
the surface, and no classical solution exists there. Filippov
\cite{Filippov1988} repairs this: on the surface the solution may
follow any convex combination of the two fields; the sliding one
cancels the normal component, and it is the limit of chattering at
infinite switching frequency. Because the plant is affine in the
elevator, combining fields equals combining actions: the
\textit{equivalent control} \cite{Utkin1977}, $\approx -5^\circ$,
an action outside the discrete set, and exactly what the blend of
Sec.~\ref{sec:4dof_solver} commands near the surface, where its
$16$ vertices mix push and pull; a mixture that pulls too hard
raises $\alpha$, shifts the query toward push vertices, and
corrects, so the closed loop finds the sliding weight by itself.
Up to the one non-affine term, the quadratic drag (a $0.6\%$
residual, under the $\pm 0.3$\,m band), the blend realizes the
Filippov solution rather than approximating it. Panel~(e) shows
that solution at finite frequency, mean deflection $-6.4^\circ$
over the arc, the classical signature of a singular arc
\cite{BrysonHo1975}; Sec.~\ref{sec:pilot_sensitivity} measures the
equivalent control independently. Only the elevator is at stake:
the throttle commands full power almost everywhere. The total loss is $19.8$\,m, and half of it is
the engine's: the same policy flown on an instantaneous propeller
loses $9.4$\,m; the optimum is simply the arm that commands power
earliest. The scripted procedures differ from the
optimum only in the power channel of panel~(f): the
$0.37$\,s FAA gating delay compounds
through the dive into a $3.9$\,m gap at recovery ($-32.2$ vs.\
$-36.1$\,m). The procedures give up $12.4$ and $16.3$\,m to the
optimum not through poor pitch flying, their pull rides the same
boundary, but through later, slower power. Absolute losses
throughout this section inherit the uncertainty of $\tau_e$,
$0.8 \pm 0.2$\,s, worth approximately $\pm 2$\,m here; the margins
\emph{between} arms are far less sensitive, and carry the
conclusions.

One entry is an anecdote.
Table~\ref{tab:montecarlo_scripted} flies the same three maneuvers
from $1000$ Latin-hypercube samples of the stalled-entry set,
through the same engine lag and with the scripted pulls capped,
entry by entry, to the pull authority the optimum commands there.
The ranking of the canonical entry is the ranking of the set: the
CAA sequence loses less altitude than the FAA sequence at
$100.0\%$ of the sampled entries, with a median advantage of
$4.8$\,m, shrinking to $0.2$\,m at the closest entry. Either
procedure gives up a median $13.5$--$18.2$\,m to the exact
optimum. The advantage
is milder than Gratton's two thirds because the scripted FAA pilot
restores power at the instant of unstall, the most favorable
reading of the FAA sequence; any recognition delay widens
the gap. At the one entry not recovered by all three maneuvers, the
slowest corner of the set
($V_0 = 0.43\,V_s$, $\alpha_0 = 18^\circ$, $q_0 = +19^\circ$/s), the
optimum and the CAA sequence recover while the FAA sequence,
waiting for the unstall before restoring power, does not. Replacing
the closed-loop pull by an open-loop full pull
($\delta_e = -25^\circ$ held) re-stalls the wing and prevents the
recovery outright, a failure mode not commensurable with power
timing and taken up in Sec.~\ref{sec:pilot_sensitivity}.

\subsection{Robustness of the Nominal Policy to Mass and CG Variations}
\label{sec:robustness}

The policy is solved once, for the nominal aircraft; the aircraft
actually flown differs from flight to flight in weight and CG
position (fuel state, payload and its placement). This subsection
flies the nominal policy, unchanged and uninformed, on the
perturbed aircraft: mass from $-15\%$ to $+15\%$ in $2.5\%$ steps
($\pm 107$\,kg on the $715$\,kg reference, a passenger with
baggage), CG from $10\%$ of chord forward to $22.5\%$ aft of the
Riley reference ($12$\,cm forward to $27$\,cm aft), nine stalled
entries per cell, $1638$ rollouts through the engine lag. The perturbation enters the dynamics only: the
observation keeps the nominal stall speed, so the policy believes
the aircraft is nominal. Entries are placed relative to the real
stall speed of the perturbed aircraft. Pitch inertia is
unperturbed, day-to-day load sits near the CG. The negative mass
range covers the AA-1's flight-to-flight envelope; the Riley
reference weight is the certificated ceiling, so the positive rows
are an overload stress case, a recurrent factor in
general-aviation accidents.

\begin{figure}[H]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.75\textwidth]{img/fig_robustness_matrix.png}
    \caption{Excess altitude loss (m) of the \emph{nominal} policy
    flown on the perturbed aircraft, relative to the same policy on
    the nominal aircraft ($-19.79$\,m, canonical entry). Positive
    (red) = additional loss. Rows are read as the weight of the
    day, columns as its placement.}
    \label{fig:robustness_matrix}
\end{figure}

Figure~\ref{fig:robustness_matrix} reports the excess loss at the
canonical entry. The matrix is smooth and monotone: mass dominates
($-13.8$\,m at $-15\%$ to $-29.8$\,m at $+15\%$, nominal CG), the
CG modulates ($+2.9$\,m at $10\%$ forward, $-3.2$\,m at $15\%$ aft:
the nose-down moment fights the pull, the aft CG relieves it). The
policy never re-stalls the aircraft: over $1638$ rollouts $\alpha$
never exceeds its entry value by more than $0.9^\circ$, because the
switching surface is set by the stall boundary of the tables and
does not move with the trim. $1635$ of $1638$ entries recover; the
three that do not are one corner, $+15\%$ mass with the CG at the
aft data edge, still descending at the $15$\,s cutoff.

The aft-CG credit is not a loading recommendation. The same shift
that relieves the pull also removes pitch stiffness once the wing
unstalls, which is why the three failures sit at the aft data
edge. And the matrix measures the cost of recovering, not the
propensity to stall: an aft-loaded aircraft is less stable and
easier to stall in the first place.

Above $+10\%$ a slower failure appears: on a $120$\,s horizon the
$+10$ and $+15\%$ rows settle into a steady powered descent
($0.27$ and $0.67$\,m/s) and never re-cross level. The descent is
a force balance: at the airspeed the policy holds, sustaining the
lift that level flight demands costs more net drag than the
embedded thrust pays, the back side of the power curve. The
failure is informational: the mis-scaled normalization hides that
the aircraft must fly faster for its weight, and the repair is
free, take-off weight is known before flight. The terminal state
is not a hazard: wings level, full throttle, shallow descent, a
condition a pilot takes over and lands from.

The ranges are the aircraft's loading decisions: half fuel is
$-4\%$ ($\approx 30$\,kg), an empty second seat $-11\%$
($\approx 80$\,kg), a light solo pilot on half tanks the $-15\%$
edge; the rows above nominal correspond to no legal loading state,
and the CG span covers every position loading can reach. The
matrix therefore spans every state this aircraft can depart in,
legal or not: the nominal policy arrests the dive in all of them,
and the one thing it can miss, the last degree of the level-off
beyond the certificated weight, is removed by one pre-flight
scalar.


\subsection{Retraining for the Perturbed Aircraft}
\label{sec:retrain}

The matrix measures the nominal policy's degradation; it cannot
say how much of that degradation is avoidable, because it never
computes the policy each perturbed aircraft deserves. This
subsection does. The policy is re-solved, at full grid, for four
loadings: mass $\pm 10\%$, the CG $10\%$ of chord forward, and the
combined stress case, $+15\%$ mass with the forward CG. Three arms
fly the same aircraft from the same true airspeed: the nominal
policy as-is (raw), the nominal policy with its stall-speed
normalization corrected from the pre-flight weight (rescaled), and
the re-solved policy $\pi_{M_1}$, the floor. Entries are deep,
$6$ to $18^\circ$ past the boundary, so every cell is a recovery
the policy must work for.

Unlike the matrix of Fig.~\ref{fig:robustness_matrix}, which
measures the perturbed aircraft against the nominal one and so
mixes the physics of the perturbation into every cell, here every
comparison is on the same aircraft: the physics cancels, and what
remains is policy error alone. That is why
Fig.~\ref{fig:robustness_matrix} reads in metres and
Fig.~\ref{fig:retrain_mass} in centimetres.
Figure~\ref{fig:retrain_mass} reports the rescaled arm against its
floor. The raw arm degrades worst where the entry is fast and the
aircraft heavy: up to $+14.1\%$ of the retrained loss at $+10\%$
mass (panel~B), $+18.6\%$ at the combined case (panel~D).
Rescaling closes nearly all
of it: no cell exceeds $+2.3\%$. The CG case (panel~C) needs no
correction
at all: the nominal policy flies within $0.3\%$ of its retrained
floor, though nothing in the observation carries the CG,
consistent with the switching surface being a property of the
aerodynamic tables rather than of the trim.

\begin{figure}[H]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.95\textwidth]{img/fig_retrain_mass.pdf}
    \caption{Excess altitude loss of the nominal policy, stall-speed
    normalization rescaled from the pre-flight weight, over the
    policy re-solved for that aircraft ($\pi_{M_1}$), at nine deep
    entries per loading: (A) $-10\%$ mass, (B) $+10\%$ mass,
    (C) CG $10\%$ of chord forward, (D) $+15\%$ mass with the
    forward CG. In parentheses, next to each entry: the altitude

Two readings of the figure need stating. The panel titles fall
with the mass, $9$ to $27$\,m in panel~A against $16$ to $39$\,m
in panel~B:
a lighter aircraft carries less energy into the dive and its
pullout is cheaper, so losing less at lower weight is the physics,
not an artifact. And several cells in panels A and C fall below
zero, a non-optimal policy beating the optimum, which \emph{is}
impossible: $\pi_{M_1}$'s optimality holds on the discretized
process, while the closed-loop evaluation adds the rollout's
integration step, the interpolated action blend, and the stopping
rule, none of which the Bellman recursion sees. The largest
negative cell is $-0.23$\,m, and that value bounds the evaluation
noise of the whole figure: every excess, positive or negative,
carries an uncertainty of that order, so a diamond within
$\approx 0.2$\,m of zero means the two policies fly the same
recovery, and its sign carries no information.

Retraining, several GPU-hours per policy, buys $0.1$ to $0.4$\,m
on recoveries costing $9$ to $46$\,m. The objection that a
tabulated optimum holds only at the point it was solved for is
answered quantitatively: the whole recoverable gap is one
pre-flight scalar, and the remainder is centimetres.



\subsection{Sensitivity to Pilot Execution Errors}
\label{sec:pilot_sensitivity}

What remains is the pilot. A human departs from the optimal
maneuver in three ways, one per phase: late power at entry, a late
switch out of the nose-down push, and a mis-flown pull once the
wing is flying again. Figure~\ref{fig:pilot_axes} measures the

perturbed. The unperturbed maneuver is the one resolved in the

acts at its start, on the power commanded at entry; panels~(b)
and~(c) act on its second half, at and after the switch marked
there at $t^\ast = 0.27$\,s: (b) mistimes the entry into the pull,
(c) mis-flies the pull that follows.

\end{figure}

\emph{Late power}, panel~(a): the mildest error, and the most
regular. The loss grows near linearly at $\approx 10$\,m per
second of delay, and the rate barely depends on the entry: a
$2$\,s delay costs $20.7$ to $21.7$\,m at all nine entries. Half a
second on the throttle is $5.8$\,m, wherever the stall began.

\emph{Late pull}, panel~(b): the steepest error of the study. The
pilot flies the push correctly but holds it $\tau_s$ beyond the
switch at $t^\ast = 0.27$\,s. Half a second multiplies the loss by
$1.4$; one second by $2.6$ ($-42$ to $-57$\,m across the grid).
The cost compounds because every instant past the switch is flown
at full power into a steepening dive.

\emph{The pull itself}, panel~(c): two failure modes, and the
asymmetry between them is the finding. The timid pilot (blue)
dares not pull harder than $\delta_e$ but keeps flying the angle
of attack; the overdone pull (red) hauls to $\delta_e$ and freezes
there. The fear is free: any ceiling from $-25$ to $-10^\circ$
reproduces the optimal loss to within $0.01$\,m, because the
sliding arc's equivalent control averages only $\approx -5^\circ$
(Sec.~\ref{sec:time_domain}) and the ceiling almost never binds.
The frozen pull is what re-stalls: it keeps pitching the aircraft
after the wing is flying again, crossing the boundary a second
time at $-7.5^\circ$ and beyond, unrecoverable within the horizon
from $-12.5^\circ$, $97.8$\,m down at full deflection. Freezing
\emph{too little} fails too, without re-stalling: $-2.5^\circ$
held loses $33.3$\,m through insufficient lift. What matters is
not how hard the pull is, but whether it is flown on the angle of
attack.

The hierarchy is then: promptness of the pull dominates
(superlinear), power timing second (linear), pull vigor free in
closed loop and catastrophic when frozen. For training the
implication is direct: initiate the pull without hesitation and
fly it on the stall boundary; the deflection used to get there is
almost immaterial. This also puts the doctrinal question of
Sec.~\ref{sec:time_domain} in its place: the entire CAA-over-FAA
margin, median $4.8$\,m over the sampled entry set, is smaller
than the cost of half a second of hesitation on the pull. What
recovery training should emphasize is not which sequence to fly,
it is how promptly and how attentively the pull is flown.

\section{Conclusions}

Exact dynamic programming for aircraft upset recovery has been
memory-bound for as long as it has existed; this work makes it
compute-bound. Integrating the transitions inside GPU registers
instead of storing them reduces the footprint from
$\mathcal{O}(N_s N_a 2^D)$ to $\mathcal{O}(N_s)$ and brings grids of
$10^7$--$10^8$ states within reach of a single GPU, while the
non-expansive barycentric interpolation keeps the discretized backup
consistent, so that what converges is the exact optimum of the
discretized process rather than a sampled approximation of it. The instrument is validated on three independent
lines: it reproduces the published value function and switching
surfaces of the 3-DOF banked-pullout benchmark \cite{Bunge2018} in $0.38$\,s end to
end, converges a $32$-million-state refinement of the same problem
(six hundred times the original grid) in under fifteen
minutes, and agrees at trajectory level with direct collocation
wherever the two formulations overlap.

Its first substantive application settles a question that certified
guidance leaves open. On a 4-DOF symmetric stall model built from
Riley's propulsion-coupled wind-tunnel tables \cite{Riley1985}, the recovery sequence
taught by the CAA \emph{emerges} from the Bellman recursion as a
property of the optimum: nothing in the formulation names a
procedure, yet the converged policy commands full power essentially
everywhere and switches from nose-down to pull on a surface pinned to
the stall angle. The resulting ordering, optimum $\le$ CAA $\le$
FAA, is certified per entry rather than on average: flown by an
identical scripted pilot through the engine lag, the CAA sequence
loses less altitude than the FAA sequence at $100.0\%$ of $1000$
Latin-hypercube samples of the four-dimensional stalled-entry set,
with a median advantage of $4.8$\,m, shrinking to $0.2$\,m at the
closest entry.

The exact solution also separates what flight test cannot. The
engine dominates: half of the optimum's own loss at the canonical
entry is thrust commanded but not yet delivered ($19.8$\,m against
$9.4$\,m on an instantaneous propeller), and either certified
procedure gives up a further median $13.5$ to $18.2$\,m to the
optimum through later, slower power, not through pitch flying. The
doctrine itself, CAA over FAA, is worth a median $4.8$\,m, milder
than the two-thirds ratio of the flight-test campaign of Gratton
et al.\ \cite{Gratton2014} because the scripted FAA pilot restores
power at the instant of unstall; any recognition delay widens the
gap.

The optimum is moreover flyable, because its structure is expressible
in the language pilots already use. The nose-down-to-pull transition
is a state event (the crossing of the stall boundary), not an
instant on a clock, and the pull that follows rides just below
$\alpha_s$ on an equivalent control of only $\approx -5^\circ$: full
power at once, push until the wing unstalls, then pull to the edge of
the usable lift and hold it there.

The sensitivity study then relocates the operational emphasis. The
promptness of that pull dominates everything, multiplying the loss
by $2.6$ per held second; power
timing is linear at $\approx 10$\,m per second; and the vigor of the
pull barely matters while it is flown on the angle of attack, yet
becomes catastrophic the moment it is not. The entire doctrinal
margin the authorities disagree over is worth less altitude than
half a second of hesitation on the pull.

The nominal policy is also robust to everything an operator cannot
control: flown unchanged across the aircraft's achievable loading
envelope it never departs controlled flight, the switching surface
survives the entire longitudinal CG range, and the heavy-overload
failures are informational rather than aerodynamic: completing the
level-off requires only that the stall speed used for normalization
reflect the weight of the day, a single pre-flight scalar. Nor is
the optimum a point solution: re-solving the policy for the
perturbed aircraft buys $0.1$ to $0.4$\,m on recoveries costing
$9$ to $46$\,m; the whole recoverable gap is that same scalar.

These claims are deliberately scoped: one airframe, one aerodynamic
model, and the symmetric regime in which the certified procedures
disagree and the flight tests were flown. The asymmetric departure
and the developed spin require the lateral--directional states, and
the compute-bound formulation is sized to reach the eight-state model
they demand. The question that opened this paper, whether the
recovery procedures aviation teaches are optimal or merely adequate,
was unanswerable for want of a reference. For the symmetric stall
of this aircraft, it is now answered; for the spin, the reference is
within reach.
