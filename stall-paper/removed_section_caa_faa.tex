\subsection{The Optimal Recovery Is the CAA Sequence: Power Timing and
Certified Procedures}
\label{sec:caa_faa}

As noted in the Introduction, certified guidance disagrees on the
timing of power during stall recovery: the CAA sequence commands full
power \emph{simultaneously} with the nose-down pitch input
\cite{CAA2009}, whereas the FAA sequence \cite{FAA2021} prescribes the
pitch reduction first, with power restored once the wing is
unstalled. In the
flight-test campaign of Gratton et al.\ \cite{Gratton2014}, the CAA
sequence consistently produced the least height loss, approximately
two thirds of the FAA figure, which in turn was about $90\%$ of a
deliberately power-delayed recovery. The exact DP solution allows this
question to be settled for the Riley model without pilot or protocol
confounds. The preceding subsection has already answered it in
structure; what follows makes the answer quantitative, on three
independent readings of the converged solution, and then measures what
the alternative sequence costs.

First, the converged
policy array itself: $99.7\%$ of the non-terminal grid states inside
the recovery-relevant window ($-10^\circ \le \alpha \le 20^\circ$,
$V \le 1.2\,V_s$, $-90^\circ \le \gamma \le 0$) command full throttle
($99.0\%$ over all non-terminal states of the $14.9$-million-state
grid), so maximum power is
not a feature of one trajectory but the argmax essentially everywhere
a recovery can evolve. Second, the time-domain structure: rollouts
from all nine initial conditions of the grid
($\alpha_0 \in \{16, 18, 20\}^\circ \times V_0/V_s \in
\{0.80, 0.85, 0.90\}$) apply full throttle from the very first control
interval, simultaneously with the elevator command; in every one, a
brief nose-down hold ($\delta_e = +15^\circ$) precedes the
pull and is terminated not at a fixed time but at a state event,
the crossing of the switching surface, reached
between $t^\ast = 0.14$ and $0.31$\,s: later the deeper and slower
the entry, exactly as a surface in state space demands. Third, the
dense sweep below: over the entire stalled-entry plane, no timing of
power ever beats the simultaneous one.

To isolate the power-timing variable, the optimal elevator schedule
was left untouched and only the throttle channel was perturbed: an
instantaneous application delayed by $\tau$; Gratton's power-delayed
protocol (PD: $\tau = 2$\,s followed by a $2$\,s ramp to full power,
replicating the flight-test procedure); and a gated variant that
withholds power until $\alpha < 14^\circ$: the FAA logic in
idealized form, with zero recognition delay.
Figures~\ref{fig:ic_optimum} and~\ref{fig:ic_procedures} report the
comparison over a dense plane of stalled entries at level entry
($\alpha_0 \in [14^\circ, 20^\circ]$,
$V_0/V_s \in [0.40, 0.90]$, $\gamma_0 = 0$, $q_0 = 0$; one
closed-loop rollout per node on a $13 \times 101$ grid, every arm
flown through the engine lag). At the canonical IC the
loss grows monotonically with any withholding of power, from
$19.8$\,m (optimal, simultaneous) to $35.3$\,m for the gated FAA
logic and $50.2$\,m for the PD protocol; the continuous delay sweep
behind that ordering is taken up, as a pilot-execution error, in
Sec.~\ref{sec:pilot_sensitivity}.
Table~\ref{tab:caa_vs_faa} reports the arms at every
initial condition of the $3 \times 3$ grid.

Figure~\ref{fig:ic_optimum} reports the optimum over that plane.
Altitude loss is governed by the energy state: the median loss runs
from $11$\,m at $V_0 = 0.90\,V_s$ to $92$\,m at $0.40\,V_s$, a span
of $81$\,m across the airspeed axis. The dependence on the depth of
the stall is weaker by well over an order of magnitude: traversing
the entire stalled range $14^\circ \le \alpha_0 \le 20^\circ$ moves
the loss by under $2$\,m at any airspeed, which is why the contours
run nearly parallel to the $\alpha_0$ axis. This is the quantitative
form of the structural result of
Sec.~\ref{sec:policy_structure}: how deeply the wing is stalled sets
how long the nose-down phase lasts, but the altitude that phase
costs is set by how much energy is missing when the recovery begins.

Figure~\ref{fig:ic_procedures} prices the procedures against that
optimum: each panel maps
$\Delta h_{\mathrm{opt}} - \Delta h_{\mathrm{proc}}$, the extra
altitude the procedure loses from the same entry, with every arm
flying the DP-optimal elevator schedule so that power timing is the
only difference between the rows. All three rows repeat one shared
scale anchored at zero excess, so the ranking is read directly down
each column. The CAA row is the floor imposed by the engine:
reaching full power through a $2$\,s ramp rather than instantaneously
costs $9$--$12$\,m (median $11.3$\,m), nearly uniformly across the
plane, and no procedure flown on this aircraft can avoid it:
everywhere in the stalled regime the aircraft needs the power it is
waiting for. The FAA row is that floor plus the price of the
doctrine, and the increment has structure: gating power on the
unstall costs most exactly where energy is scarcest, a median of
$2.8$\,m at $V_0 = 0.90\,V_s$ growing to $18.9$\,m at $0.40\,V_s$
($7.4$\,m over the plane), because the slower the entry, the longer
the wing takes to unstall and the longer the gate withholds the
power. A single headline number therefore understates the penalty
exactly where altitude margin matters most. Most of what either
certified sequence gives up to the optimum is nevertheless the
engine, not the doctrine, a distinction that bounds how much any
revision of the published procedure could recover. The power-delayed
row puts both in perspective: Gratton's deliberate $2$\,s pause
costs $28$--$31$\,m over the optimum, dwarfing the engine floor and
the doctrine effect alike.

Across the $1313$ nodes of the plane, the ordering
$|\Delta h_{\mathrm{opt}}| \le |\Delta h_{\mathrm{CAA}}| \le
|\Delta h_{\mathrm{FAA}}|$ holds without exception, and
$|\Delta h_{\mathrm{FAA}}| \le |\Delta h_{\mathrm{PD}}|$ at all but
three nodes in the lowest-energy corner ($V_0 = 0.40\,V_s$,
$\alpha_0 \ge 19^\circ$), where the wing takes longer than
Gratton's $2$\,s clock to unstall, the gate opens after it, and the
two arms coincide to within $0.2$\,m. The exception proves the
mechanism: the FAA gate \emph{is} a delay, of whatever duration the
unstall happens to take.

Both figures are confined to $\gamma_0 = q_0 = 0$: one plane of a
four-dimensional entry set. Table~\ref{tab:montecarlo} removes that
restriction: every arm is flown from the same $1000$ Latin-hypercube
samples spanning all four entry states
($\gamma_0 \in [-30^\circ, 0]$, $V_0/V_s \in [0.40, 0.90]$,
$\alpha_0 \in [14^\circ, 20^\circ]$, $q_0 \in [-20, 20]^\circ$/s),
so the interactions the planar maps cannot show are included.
The ranking survives intact: the CAA sequence loses less than the
FAA sequence at $100\%$ of the sampled entries, by a median of
$5.9$\,m. But the certificate also records how thin the margin
becomes at its narrowest, $0.18$\,m, which no aggregate figure would
reveal.

The ratios, not the absolute losses, are what may be compared
against the flight tests, and the comparison must respect what each
arm idealizes: the gated arm is the FAA logic with zero recognition
delay, so the FAA sequence as actually flown, with a human check in
the loop, lies between it and the power-delayed protocol. Gratton's
protocol decelerates at $1$\,kt/s to the natural stall break
($V \lesssim V_s$), which corresponds to the fast edge of the
mapped plane. Under matched power-ramp modeling, the simultaneous
sequence loses $0.52$--$0.54$ of the power-delayed figure along the
$V_0 = 0.90\,V_s$ row, against the $\approx 0.6$ implied by the
flight-test chain (CAA $\approx \tfrac{2}{3} \times$ FAA
$\approx 0.9\times$ PD). Notably, Gratton's fleet included the
Grumman AA-5A, a direct sibling of the AA-1 modeled here, which lost
$120$\,ft under the CAA sequence against $230$\,ft under the flown
FAA: a ratio of $0.52$.
